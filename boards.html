Educational Objectives

Robotics & Embedded Systems — Boards Portfolio

Boards is a separate graduation portfolio from my Student Innovation Project (SIP). Each of the six program objectives requires evidence from two distinct projects. A single project may support no more than two objectives. The assignments below are proposed evidence selections, not yet faculty-approved completions.

Boards Objectives and Project Evidence

Each objective is shown word for word, followed by two projects explaining how their work supports it. Projects may be used for at most two objectives. Some matches are provisional; incomplete work is labeled clearly.

Objective 1

Design and complete robotic and embedded systems solutions that apply to real-world situations and challenges.

Project 1: Well Killer SIP

I applied embedded system design to the real-world challenge of shutting down distant irrigation wells during severe weather. I integrated Arduino controllers, a Nextion touchscreen, LoRa communications, and a relay interface. Bench tests verified command transmission, relay activation, and simulated feedback, demonstrating an engineered response to a specific agricultural problem. Connection to a running engine has not yet been validated.

Project 2: Mini Robotic Arm

I designed and assembled a 3D-printed robotic arm around an Arduino Uno, motors, joystick controls, and sensing components. I combined mechanical construction, electrical wiring, and firmware, then tested subsystems and resolved power and wiring faults. This demonstrates an end-to-end embedded robotics solution applicable to small-scale manipulation; the assembled stepper shoulder has not been fully load-validated.

Objective 2

Implement a simple microprocessor using digital logic design.

Project 1: Arduino Simon Says Game

I programmed the Arduino Uno ATmega328P to read digital buttons, generate and store sequences, drive LED outputs, and compare input against the stored pattern. This demonstrates processor-controlled digital logic, state sequencing, and awareness of limited embedded memory. The project programs an existing microcontroller rather than constructing a processor from logic gates.

Project 2: 4-Bit Hexadecimal Seven-Segment Display

I implemented binary input interpretation and hexadecimal output decoding: four digital inputs form a 4-bit value, and software selects the corresponding active-low segment pattern. This provides direct evidence of digital logic concepts and microcontroller I/O implementation. Together with Simon Says, it demonstrates introductory processor and digital-logic skills, not a custom-built CPU.

Objective 3

Demonstrate embedded system design skills, including, but not limited to, microcontroller selection, schematic design, printed circuit board layout, design for electromagnetic compatibility and design for manufacturing.

Project 1: Well Killer SIP

This demonstrates microcontroller selection, system-level wiring design, and consideration of installation constraints. Objective 4

Apply knowledge of transducers, actuators and simultaneous hardware and software development in the design of an embedded system.

Project 1: Motor Testing and PWM Control Lab

I interfaced the Arduino Uno with an L293D H-bridge to drive two DC motor actuators, and developed firmware to change direction and ramp speed with PWM. I tested electrical control and motor behavior together, demonstrating simultaneous hardware and software development. The control was open-loop rather than sensor-feedback regulated.

Project 2: Potentiometer and Photoresistor Lab

I used the Arduino ADC to convert potentiometer and photoresistor signals into values that control LED outputs, with a switch selecting the active sensor. This demonstrates transducer input acquisition, calibration and mapping of sensor readings, and coordinated circuit and program development. Combined with the motor lab, the two projects cover sensing and actuation.
